Learning Khmer with AI

KhmerEnglish

I've spent most of my schooling trying to understand how computing impacts cultural heritage. Lately, I've spent a bit of time reflecting on what AI is doing to my own cultural heritage and what to do about it. Here's some very drafty thoughts.

Visiting Cambodia meant constantly being interrogated about my Khmerness by the locals. “អូនចេះខ្មែរទេ?” (Do you speak Khmer?) they would ask.

My Cambodian-born parents, worried about my English proficiency in the United States, raised me speaking more English than Khmer. I don’t blame them; they were only in elementary school when the Khmer Rouge forced them into rural labor camps in 1975. Born decades later, I found myself code-switching during family gatherings and visits to the wat, much like many second-generation Cambodian Americans. My cousins and friends could speak some Khmer, but most of us were illiterate due to social pressures and English-only language policies. After college, I began formally learning Khmer while documenting my ancestry, realizing that English-only approaches could not capture the complexity of my Khmerican identity.

High-quality Khmer learning resources for English speakers are scarce. When I started looking for them, I discovered that there were very few comprehensive textbooks. Most language-learning platforms did not support Khmer—or worse, used an underdeveloped and erroneous curriculum. I looked for Khmer courses at my alma mater, only to find the program sunsetting due to budget cuts (Goh, 2026). So, I started evaluating community-based courses, in which the quality of instruction greatly varied. I stumbled upon an online course hosted by Cambodian American volunteers developing the curriculum for ReanKhmer, an AI language-learning site built for the Khmer diaspora (Chea Labs, 2026). I signed up, thrilled to have found a new resource created by Cambodians. Could AI be used to teach Khmer in a culturally responsive way?

I was aware of the opportunities and limitations associated with AI in language-learning contexts. In college, I read about creative approaches to automated speech recognition (McCrocklin, 2016; Sun, 2023), translation tools (Muñoz-Basols, 2023), conversational AI (Ji et al., 2023; Wang, 2024), and numerous other techniques in natural language processing (NLP) seeking to improve the learning process. These technologies often sit in tension with the human connection that language learning facilitates. For example, a conversational AI could decrease learners’ anxiety, but it might not provide appropriate corrective feedback to raise awareness of errors. Unintentionally, transcription models might bias towards mainstream accents, failing to acknowledge the dialectal variation across a language. Regarding the cultural values embedded in language, preliminary studies on large language models have found that outputs are skewed towards Western values (Arora et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2025). The implications are largely unknown for Khmer, a language underrepresented in second language acquisition research and in the datasets used to train language models.

I was terrified by the thought of an anthropomorphized AI transforming the way I interacted with my heritage. When going from consuming Khmer content to producing it, would my words feel authentic and human? Or would they be marked by language model mistakes, optimistic oversimplifications, and emotional misalignment?

I asked ReanKhmer’s Anthropic-powered AI tutor to tell me how it worked. The following response was returned:

សួស្តី! (Sustei!) Hello! I'm so glad you asked! Think of me as your personal Khmer language coach - like having a patient aunt or uncle who helps you reconnect with your Cambodian heritage.

This familial persona made me think back to the grandparents, aunts, and uncles missing from my life. My parents lost the majority of their immediate family during the Khmer Rouge period. The prospect of a chatbot filling these absences in my family’s memory was disturbing. Furthermore, unlike the insights coming from a “patient aunt or uncle,” anything that the AI tutor generated had to be rigorously fact-checked against reputable sources. Textbooks articulated the explicit definitions I yearned for. The Cambodian teachers aided my contextual understanding through culturally informed examples. My parents taught me how to express my ideas the Cambodian American way. In the end, the most useful prompts I sent to the AI tutor were ones that successfully retrieved the proper linguistic terminology for Khmer script structures that I haphazardly described.

The ReanKhmer platform also had options to create AI-generated cultural stories in English for educational purposes. Stories spoke of wonder, resilience, and legacy but repeatedly failed to mention trauma, sacrifice, and healing. One story concluded, “From the churning of the Ocean of Milk to the royal courts of Angkor, from the ancient bas-reliefs carved in stone to the living dancers who grace stages today—this is your inheritance, woven through centuries like golden thread.” Having AI-generated texts attempt to embody oral historians evoked an unsettling combination of subjunctive nostalgia (Malek, 2021) and ethical dilemmas in historical representation. Who gets to tell these stories? What kinds of stories should we allow AI to generate? These are questions that Cambodians must wrestle with. Reading the origin stories left me with one recurring thought regarding these memory politics. We need Cambodians to write these, not AI!

Given the immense sociocultural context surrounding heritage learners and Khmer’s classification as an under resourced language in NLP, my experience with other general language AI tools was unsurprisingly similar. Software companies have been working on Khmer language transcription, but gaps in linguistic knowledge, data availability, and model development continue to pose challenges. An article published in Cambodian news outlet Cambodianess detailed how Apple’s optical character recognition system mistranslated Khmer into Thai, creating linguistic preservation problems (Kongieng, 2026). Khmer content circulating online is largely audio, attributed to language accessibility issues in digital technology (Elliott & Phorn, 2021). Sometimes I would watch YouTube’s automatic captioning algorithm attempt to transcribe unmistakably Khmer audio into a nonsensical string of Thai or Vietnamese characters. ReanKhmer’s underlying speech model for pronunciation feedback had a long way to go to before it matched the utility of its English-language counterparts. Current “state-of-the-art” systems leave much to be desired.

Accessibility remains a large barrier to learning, and I can see human-verified AI content benefiting the Khmer diaspora in some cases. I hope to see more community-driven initiatives to build datasets, software, and AI models with care and sustainability at the forefront. ReanKhmer’s development was accelerated with AI to generate code and content, a tradeoff between delivery speed and human design thinking. This necessitates guidelines around how and where AI is used in the process of creating learning materials, beyond defining how students should use the explicitly labeled interactive AI features. The platform simultaneously exemplified how a passionate Cambodian community of technologists and teachers can come together to co-create digital resources for the Khmer language. Its human-recorded audio, script-tracing exercises, and beginner-friendly podcasts made my learning experience engaging and approachable. While I have much to say about digital interactions, the most valuable part of the platform was the people behind the lessons.

The Khmer community motivated me to stick with learning the language. For almost half a year, I spent an hour a day reviewing Franklin Huffman’s Cambodian System of Writing and Beginning Reader, five hours a week on the ReanKhmer platform, and a mortifying quantity of time binge-watching Khmer videos produced by both foreigners and Cambodian content creators. When I visited Cambodia the following summer, I found myself reading, writing, and speaking Khmer with a proficiency I could have never fathomed a year prior. I ordered food off Cambodian menus in Phnom Penh. I understood the Cambodian tour guides in Battambang. I read my grandmother’s Khmer name on a building when visiting my father’s birthplace in Takeo. As I learned the language of my ancestors, I learned about myself.

Being second-generation Cambodian American makes me a cultural broker, handling the linguistic and cultural barriers that arise when navigating different social systems. My formal computer science and language training ingrains a critical view of technology within me. This background inevitably shapes my responses to AI and language learning as an end user. My reactions are predicated on the transcultural space I inhabit, suspended between Cambodian and American values in one dimension, and computational and humanistic methods of inquiry in another. At the same time, I have realized that this intersectionality places me in a unique position of privilege to shape how I am represented in computing and what the future of heritage language learning entails.

I don’t want these tools to replace my thinking and tell my stories. I don't want to talk to an AI uncle either. I don't want large language models to tell me what it means to be Khmer. But what if emerging technologies could make us more critical thinkers and better storytellers? What if AI systems could mobilize language learners and educators to create and connect with one another? What if interactions were designed to be more transparent and thoughtful, providing learners with the agency over how they use AI? Systems need to break in the right way. Space needs to be left for refusal. The communities that technology is built for need to be involved in shaping them as they are developed. That technology needs to be designed to promote ethical usage from its inception.

Although initial experiences have left me conflicted, I am hopeful about the responsible use of AI in heritage language learning when integrated with other teaching approaches and better AI literacy education. I remain deeply empathetic towards the desire for better Khmer learning resources. For a long time, I have believed that Khmer Americans should be visible in the schools they attend, books they read, and technology they use. With language central to a sense of belonging, the stakes are high for Cambodian descendants around the world working to understand and preserve the stories they grew up with. The erasure that AI can cause in Cambodian history and identity is alarming. One of the most promising paths I see for these technologies is to activate the community to create counternarratives that reclaim what it means to be from the Khmer diaspora. But I am still figuring out what exactly that entails. For now, I'll continue to dream of the day when any កូនខ្មែរ (Khmer child) can proudly say, “ខ្ញុំអាចនិយាយភាសាខ្មែរបាន” (I can speak Khmer).
